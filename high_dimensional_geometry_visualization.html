High-Dimensional Geometry

Step 1 of 5

The Unit Sphere

To understand the strange properties of high-dimensional space, we first ground our intuition in a familiar setting: the 3D unit sphere.

Equation of the sphere:

$$x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = 1$$

Notice the horizontal axis is labelled \(x_1\). Imagine we pick a uniformly random point on this surface. We want to study its behavior by focusing purely on its first coordinate, \(x_1\).

Slicing the Sphere

Let's fix the first coordinate at a specific value, \(x_1 = t\). Geometrically, this means we are cutting the sphere with a vertical plane.

The resulting cross-section satisfies:

$$x_2^2 + x_3^2 = 1 - t^2$$

This is a circle with radius \(r = \sqrt{1 - t^2}\).

Comparing Cross-Sections

Observe how the size of the cross-section changes depending on where we slice along the \(x_1\)-axis.

Probability & Surface Area

Consider a thin band between \(x_1 = t\) and \(x_1 = t + \Delta t\). The probability that a random point lands in this band is proportional to its surface area.

The Geometric Intuition

A larger cross-section means the band has a wider circumference, allowing it to encompass more points. Since the absolute largest cross-section is exactly at \(t=0\), points have a tendency to cluster near the equator.

The Phenomenon in d-Dimensions

In 3D, this shrinking effect is mild. But let's look at what happens in \(d\) dimensions (where \(d\) is very large, e.g., 1000).

In 3D

Cross-section is a circle (1-dimensional boundary). Size scales directly with \(r = \sqrt{1-t^2}\).

In d-Dimensions

Fixing \(x_1=t\) yields a \((d-2)\)-dimensional sphere: $$x_2^2 + \dots + x_d^2 = 1-t^2$$

Its size scales by \(r^{d-2} = (1-t^2)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\).

The Conclusion:

As \(d \to \infty\), the term \((1-t^2)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\) crushes down to zero exponentially fast for any \(t \neq 0\). Thus, virtually 100% of the surface area—and probability—is concentrated in an infinitesimally thin band around the equator \(x_1 = 0\).

Any Direction: Vector \(u\)

There is nothing special about the first coordinate \(x_1\). Imagine choosing any direction vector \(u\) of unit length (\(\|u\| = 1\)).

Instead of focusing on \(x_1\), we ask about the projection \(\langle x, u \rangle\). How far does a random point \(x\) extend in the direction of \(u\)?

Rotational Symmetry

Because the sphere is perfectly rotationally symmetric, we can mentally rotate our coordinate system so that \(u\) becomes our new \(x_1\) axis. Therefore, \(\langle x, u \rangle\) behaves exactly like \(x_1\)!

The Thin Slab

The equation \(\langle x, u \rangle = 0\) defines a hyperplane passing through the origin. Think of this as the new "equator" perpendicular to \(u\).

The remarkable high-dimensional fact:

$$\langle x, u \rangle \text{ is typically } \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)$$

The region \(|\langle x, u \rangle| \le \frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\) forms a thin slab around this hyperplane. Even though this slab becomes thinner as \(d\) increases, it contains an overwhelmingly large fraction of the ball's volume!
